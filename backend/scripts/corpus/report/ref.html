The fallback chain, tested leg by leg

Not read from config — every leg given a real call and timed.

In plain English: we rent AI from six suppliers, in order. If the first cannot answer we drop to the second, and so on down. That list is the difference between a slow answer and no answer at all.

Nobody had ever checked that the list actually works. I gave every leg one tiny question and timed the reply. Half of them cannot answer, and the last one — the safety net — was never even loaded.

The safety net was removed without telling anyoneFixed
OPENAI_BASE_URL points at models.inference.ai.azure.com — the retired GitHub Models host, which no longer resolves. config.ts drops all four OpenAI legs when it sees that, which is correct: calling a dead host would only add latency. But it did it in silence, so a chain that had lost its last resort looked identical to a healthy one right up until everything above it was spent. That is the 25% outage. The drop now logs a warning at startup naming the URL, the consequence and both fixes.
Intent extraction was walking a different chainFixed
Every turn extracts intent before it answers, and that step kept its own hand-written copy of the provider order. The copy had drifted: it omitted GEMINI_API_KEY1 entirely, so with the billed key out of credits — its state right now — intent extraction skipped the one working Gemini key and jumped to Mistral. It also asked Cerebras for a different model than the answer chain did, so the two could fail differently in the same outage. Now derived from FALLBACK_CHAIN, which is what CLAUDE.md required all along.
A test caught the consequence immediatelyGood
Deriving the intent chain broke intent-extraction-fallback.test.ts: its helper removed 7 provider keys, but the derived chain has 13, so GROQ_API_KEY2 survived, answered, and the test read a real provider call as the local fast path. The complete key list already existed beside it. This is what a test earning its place looks like — it failed for the right reason on a change that looked safe.

Three new tests lock the contract: every leg names a provider the intent path can actually call, at least three distinct providers remain in the chain, and no free-tier key ever ranks above a billed one — the ordering rule that exists because a free key at position two once caught every failure of the paid one and turned a recoverable stall into an empty reply.

The six ideas taken

1 · Score answers on separate axes Built promptflow/examples/flows/evaluation
gpt_retrieval_score 1–5 are the retrieved documents relevant gpt_groundedness 1–10 is the answer supported by the context gpt_generation does the answer address the question

Why it applies. Our corpus runner has said in its own header, for weeks, that grading there is mechanical and "leaves quality judgement to a separate judge pass". That pass never existed — so the only quality reading we ever had came from a person reading answers one at a time, which is exactly how 120 answers got reported as 100% correct on a day a quarter of them were outages.

What was built. npm run judge scores a saved results file on three axes: relevance, usefulness, honesty. It runs against a file, never live, so it costs nothing until you choose and re-running is free to repeat. Outages are excluded — averaging them in makes the model look worse while hiding the availability problem behind a quality number.

What it found immediately. Over the last real run: relevance 3.60, usefulness 1.40, honesty 4.60. It also caught something I had read as a good answer — "asked for 4 BHK but provided 4.5 BHK layouts".

2 · Every claim traces to a source Built Perplexity/deep-research.md
Add a citation to every sentence that includes information derived from tool outputs.

Why it applies. A citation rule is really a promise that every claim traces to something the system was handed. Our sources are database rows, and buyers would not read markers — but the underlying test is mechanical: a rupee figure, an area or a year in the reply that appears nowhere in the prompt is one the model supplied from memory.

What was built. groundingCheck.ts pulls every figure out of an answer and looks for it in the exact prompt that produced it. Statutory rates are exempt — 7% stamp duty is UP law and needs no row behind it. It logs, never blocks: arithmetic we asked for legitimately produces figures absent from context, so the false-positive rate has to be measured first.

It caught its own author before it caught the product. The first version anchored on ₹ immediately followed by a unit, so ₹1.25–1.85 Cr — our own commonest price format — matched nothing and an invented range passed as grounded. Both ends of a range are now checked, with a test for that exact case.

3 · Formatting rules, stated exactly Built Perplexity/perplexity-ai.md
Always begin your final response with content, not a header. Markdown tables are not for summaries. All bullets must be top-level. Never indent bullets under other bullets. Do not add follow-up questions for other types of queries.

Each line names something we measured. Across 120 answers: 30 opened with a heading or a bare | Parameter | Value | table, 14 wrapped a single value in a three-line table, and follow-up questions were appended to plain factual replies. Perplexity restricts follow-ups to writing and rewriting requests — that is now our rule too.

All four are in the per-question output contract. The judge score above is the baseline they have to beat.

4 · A named phrase for missing data Aligned search_with_lepton/search_with_lepton.py
Say "information is missing on" followed by the related topic, if the given context do not provide sufficient information.

This is the rule you asked for in your own words — if we cannot get something, say so plainly rather than substituting a number. Lepton makes it a specific phrase the model must produce, which is stricter than a general instruction not to guess.

We already had the mechanism (unverified(), the four fact tiers, "Not recorded" in rendered cells). What we lacked was the model being told which words to use, so it improvised — sometimes well, sometimes with a spec dump. The contract now names the behaviour.

5 · Follow-ups must stand alone Already fixed today search_with_lepton/search_with_lepton.py
if the original question asks about "the Manhattan project", in the follow up question, do not just say "the project", but use the full name "the Manhattan project"

This is precisely the chip bug. A follow-up that says "this project" or "I need a 3 BHK" cannot be asked on its own — the buyer taps it and the system has lost what "this" meant. Lepton states the rule generally; we had found the symptom this morning and fixed it (chips now name the project, and a lone filter chip returns nothing at all).

Worth recording as independent confirmation that the fix was the right shape, and that the rule generalises beyond chips to anything we suggest.

6 · Batch API for evaluation runs Recommended cookbook/quickstarts/Batch_mode.ipynb
Cost savings: Batches are priced at a 50% discount compared to the standard API. Note: The Batch API is a separate endpoint that works independently from both generateContent and the Interactions API. Ideal for … running large-scale evaluations.

This is aimed at exactly what you are paying for tonight. Google's own words: ideal for large-scale evaluations. Half price, and — the part that matters more — a separate endpoint with its own quota. Our corpus runs would stop competing with real users for the same allowance.

I have not built the integration: it needs file upload, job polling and a 24-hour completion window, and shipping an untested API integration the night you plan to run the corpus is the wrong order. Recommended as the next infrastructure step, not tonight's.

The thing the judge measured, in plain English

What the three numbers mean. Imagine grading an estate agent on three separate things instead of one overall mark.

Relevance 3.6/5 — did they answer the question you asked? Mostly yes.

Honesty 4.6/5 — did they only say things they actually knew? Almost always. This is the one that protects buyers, and it is our strongest score.

Usefulness 1.4/5 — did they help you decide, or just read the file out? They read the file out. This is the number behind every complaint you have had this week about answers looking like a database.

Grading them separately is the whole point of the promptflow approach. A single "quality: 65%" would have hidden this: the answers are correct and honest and still not useful, and only one of those three needs fixing. Averaging them would have pointed us at the wrong work.

$ npm run judge results-longtail2.json relevance 3.60 answers the question asked usefulness 1.40 advises rather than dumps fields honesty 4.60 claims only what it can know [5/1/1] ace hanei construction update Unverifiable construction data, bare table, no context [3/1/5] ace hanei floor plan Gives a price table instead of actual floor plan layouts [3/5/5] ace hanei 4 bhk Asked for 4 BHK but provided 4.5 BHK layouts

Those scores are the baseline, measured on answers produced before today's fixes — before the invented price table was removed from the prompt and before the four formatting rules were added. Tonight's run is the comparison, and usefulness is the number to watch.

What the study found by accident

The 25% outage is not a rate limit. The first judge run came back on every single call with: "Your prepayment credits are depleted. Please go to AI Studio…" — not a throttle, an empty account. That is a more precise diagnosis than the corpus run's 429s suggested, and it means topping up tonight genuinely fixes it, rather than merely raising a ceiling we would hit again.

The judge itself now takes JUDGE_GEMINI_KEY so it can run on a spare or free-tier key — an evaluator should never compete with the product for the billed account.

What the portals taught by having nothing to teach

In plain English: I looked at how six property sites show a price. Every one shows a single number, because on a portal a listing is one flat. We show a project — many flats at many prices — so one number is either wrong or meaningless.

That is not a gap in our design. It is the reason yesterday's work matters: quoting one price for a whole project is a portal habit that does not survive an advisor's job.

The relevant code in all six was one field rendered directly — ₹{price.toLocaleString()}. No configuration-scoped pricing exists in any of them because none of them needs it. Our BHK-scoped price and the "this project has no 3 BHK" answer have no equivalent in any portal examined.

What I deliberately did not take

  • Inline [1][2] markers in answers. Perplexity's readers expect them; a home buyer reading advice does not. The check is worth having, the notation is not.
  • Onyx's "use tables for structured information" default. Directly contrary to what we measured — tables were 53% of output tokens and the main reason answers read like a database. Their context is enterprise document search; ours is advice.
  • Perplexity's personalisation block (demographics, lifestyle, shopping patterns). IntentState already carries the equivalent, scoped to what a property decision needs. Importing a general profile would collect more about a buyer than the job requires.
  • astack and budibase as frameworks. Both are sound; adopting either is a rewrite, not a refinement, and you asked me not to disturb what works.
  • The four chat UI shells. 20,506 files of chat interface. Our constraint is whether the answer is right, not what surrounds it.

The bottom line

The references confirmed more than they corrected. Our conditional output contract, our choke-point sanitising and our fact tiers all match what production systems arrived at independently.

Three genuine gaps, and they are the same gap wearing different clothes: nothing checked itself.

Attribution — nothing required a claim to trace to a row, and the cost showed up as a hardcoded price table asserting figures wrong by more than half. Measurement — nothing scored an answer's usefulness, so the complaint you kept raising had no number and no way to prove it fixed. Availability — nothing ever asked the fallback chain whether it worked, so it quietly lost its safety net and looked healthy until the day everything above it ran out.

All three now have a command behind them that fails loudly. One found a fault in an answer I had personally graded as good; another found that half the providers you believed were standing behind this product are not.
